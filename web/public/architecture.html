0. What this is, in one paragraph

This is a quant research and execution platform for Indian equity markets, built for one person trading their own money. It downloads market history, cleans it, turns it into features, tests trading strategies against it, subjects the survivors to statistical tests designed to catch self-deception, and — if anything survives — places the orders. The whole system is organised around a single uncomfortable fact: it is very easy to produce a trading strategy that looks excellent and is worthless. Most of the code exists to make that harder, not to make strategies.

1. The one idea you need first

If you take nothing else from this document, take this.

Suppose you test 20 random, meaningless strategies. Each is pure noise. The best of those 20 will still look good — not because it works, but because you looked 20 times. With enough attempts, an impressive backtest is the expected outcome of nothing at all.

This is not a small effect. It is the dominant effect. It is why the system:

  • counts every strategy ever evaluated in a tamper-evident ledger, and

raises the bar for all future strategies as that count grows (§5.3);

  • splits data in ways that stop information leaking from the test set into

the training set (§5.1);

  • refuses to let you tune a failed strategy and resubmit it (§5.8);
  • reads its final holdout data exactly once, ever (§5.8).

Everything else is plumbing in service of that idea.

The single most important structural fact: a strategy is handed a MarketView object containing only history up to its decision date. It cannot see the future because the future is not in the object it was given. This is called the lookahead firewall, and it is structural rather than a convention — nothing has to remember to respect it.

Part I — Data

3.1 The landing zone: raw data is immutable

Downloaded files land in data/landing/ and are never edited and never deleted. Every file is hashed on arrival; make check-landing re-hashes them and proves nothing changed.

Why so strict? Because every number downstream is derived from these files. If a raw file can be quietly "fixed", then a result can change without any code changing, and no investigation will ever find out why. Immutable raw data means any result is reproducible from first principles.

Local disk is not the storage of record — a Backblaze B2 bucket is (23,230 objects, 4.45 GB). Local copies are pruned once B2 confirms the upload.

3.2 The bitemporal model: two clocks, not one

This is the most important data concept in the system, and the one most commonly got wrong elsewhere.

Every stored record carries two timestamps:

fieldmeaning
event_timewhen the thing happened in the world
knowledge_timewhen we could first have known it

A third, receipt_time, records when we actually downloaded it.

Why two clocks matter. Suppose a company announces on 15 March that a dividend went ex on 1 March. The event_time is 1 March. The knowledge_time is 15 March. A backtest standing on 5 March must not see it, because nobody could have. Using event_time alone would leak the future into the past — an error that silently inflates returns.

The read API enforces this:

query(as_of) returns exactly those records where knowledge_time ≤ as_of.

as_of is a required, keyword-only argument. You cannot forget it, because the function will not run without it. This is what "structural, not conventional" means in practice (ADR-014).

A real mistake this caught. An early version used the file's download time as knowledge_time. The median gap between the real announcement and our download was 1,820 days — five years. Every backtest built on it was silently reading the future.

3.3 The security master: what is an instrument?

Symbols are not identity. Companies rename (ZOMATO → ETERNAL), merge, demerge and delist. If a backtest joins data on a ticker, it will silently combine two different companies' histories.

So the system assigns an internal instrument ID and joins on it, never on a ticker or a broker token. Instrument attributes are time-versioned: asking "what was NIFTY's lot size on this date?" returns the value that was true then.

Measured from our own data, NIFTY's lot size was 50 (from 2024-03), 25 (2024-11), 75 (2025-03), 65 (2026-03). Hardcoding any one of those would corrupt every derivative calculation outside its window.

A real mistake this caught. Joining corporate actions on a raw ISIN string lost 21.9% of matches. Joining on the internal ID recovered them, which changed adjustment-factor coverage from 78.1% to 98.1% and recovered 3,073 dividend factors that had been silently dropped.

3.4 Corporate actions: the mathematics of adjustment

When a company splits its stock 1-for-1, the price halves overnight. A holder loses nothing — they now own twice as many shares — but a naive price series shows a −50% crash.

Price adjustment

For each corporate action we compute an adjustment factor f. For a bonus or split where each old share becomes r new shares:

    f = r

A 1:1 bonus gives f = 2.0. To make historical prices comparable with today's, every price before the ex-date is divided by the cumulative product of all factors that occur after it:

    P_adjusted(t) = P_raw(t) / ∏ f_i     for all events i with ex_date_i > t

Verified against real data: 40 of 40 genuine 1:1 bonuses show the expected ~2× raw price drop, which the factor of 2.0 exactly corrects.

Dividends and total return

A dividend D paid on a stock trading at P also drops the price. For total-return series (which assume dividends are reinvested), the factor is:

    f_dividend = P_cum / (P_cum − D)

where P_cum is the last price at which the stock traded with the dividend attached ("cum-dividend").

Rights issues

A rights issue lets holders buy new shares at a discount, which dilutes the price. The correct divisor is the Theoretical Ex-Rights Price (TERP):

                N · P_cum  +  M · S
    TERP  =  ─────────────────────────
                     N + M

    f_rights = P_cum / TERP

where N = existing shares, M = new shares offered, S = subscription price. This was cross-checked algebraically against the standard formula and validated against Reliance's 2020 rights issue.

The subtlety: two different adjusted series

The system uses two adjusted price series, and conflating them causes a severe bug:

  • For signals — prices adjusted as of the decision date, using only

actions knowable then. A strategy deciding on 2018-01-15 must not see a bonus that goes ex in May.

  • For marking positions — prices adjusted uniformly as of the end of the

run, so a position's value does not jump on the day an adjustment updates.

A real mistake this caught. Marking positions from the raw series booked an 89% phantom loss on VIVIDHA (1,290.55 → 140.75 across an 11:1 action) and dragged an eleven-year buy-and-hold result to −3.3%. The holder had lost nothing.

3.5 Data quality: why the median, not the mean

To detect a corrupt price, you need a measure of "normal spread". The obvious choice — standard deviation — is a trap, because the corrupt values themselves inflate it, hiding the very thing you are looking for.

The technical term is breakdown point: the fraction of data that can be arbitrarily wrong before the statistic becomes meaningless. Standard deviation and the mean have a breakdown point of 0 — a single bad value can move them without limit.

So the system uses Median Absolute Deviation (MAD), breakdown point 0.5:

    MAD  =  median( | x_i − median(x) | )

To make it readable on a familiar scale, it is multiplied by 1.4826 (which is 1/Φ⁻¹(0.75)), so that for genuinely normal data it matches the standard deviation:

                   x_i − median(x)
    robust_z  =  ────────────────────
                  1.4826 · MAD

A point is flagged when |robust_z| > 3.5. The scaling only affects how the number reads — never which points are flagged relative to each other.

The same principle appears elsewhere: Theil–Sen regression instead of ordinary least squares (breakdown point 0.29 vs 0), and Spearman rank correlation instead of Pearson.

Part II — Research

4.1 Features and the Information Coefficient

A feature is a number computed per stock per day that you hope predicts future returns — for example, "12-month return, skipping the last month" (momentum_12_1). The library has ten, each oriented so higher is better.

To ask whether a feature predicts anything, compute the Information Coefficient (IC): on each date, the rank correlation between the feature and the return that followed.

    IC_t  =  Spearman( f_{i,t} ,  r_{i,t→t+h} )

where f_{i,t} is the feature for stock i on date t, and r is the return over the next h days (default 21). Spearman = Pearson correlation of the ranks, which is insensitive to outliers and to any monotonic rescaling.

You then get a time series of ICs, one per date. Its significance:

                mean(IC)
    t  =  ──────────────────────
           std(IC) / √T

with T the number of dates. A typical genuine equity signal has a mean IC around 0.02–0.05 — small, and that is normal. What matters is consistency.

Warning attached to this project's own IC figures: the Sprint 8 IC numbers were computed on raw, unadjusted prices over a 3-year panel and have not been recomputed (ticket T-1317). They should not be relied on.

4.2 The cost model: itemised, not blended

Trading costs in India are not one number. They are a stack of separate charges, each with different rules about which side pays. Blending them into "0.1% round trip" is wrong in both directions.

For a trade of Q shares at price P, turnover T = P × Q:

chargerate (equity delivery)falls on
Brokerage0 (delivery); min(0.03%, ₹20) otherwiseboth
STT (Securities Transaction Tax)0.1% of turnoverboth, on delivery
Stamp duty0.015%buy only
Exchange transaction charge~0.00325%both
SEBI turnover fee0.0001%both
GST18%on brokerage + exchange charges only

So:

    GST  =  0.18 × (brokerage + exchange_charges)

— not on turnover, and not on STT. Getting this wrong is a common error.

Two properties of the implementation matter:

  1. Rates are time-versioned. STT on futures changed 4× inside the

backtest window. A hardcoded rate would misprice years of history.

  1. Everything is Decimal, not float. Contract notes reconcile to the

paisa, and a float that is out by 1e-9 fails that comparison for reasons unrelated to correctness.

Unverified: every rate comes from a secondary source and has never been checked against a real contract note (T-906, A-016). This is the cheapest outstanding verification in the project.

4.3 Two backtest engines, and why

Tier A (vectorised)Tier B (event-driven)

Tier A is explicitly not a backtest (ADR-021) — it is a screen. It cannot model an order that was rejected because the stock did not trade that day.

Both engines run the same Strategy object, unchanged. This is a hard rule: one interface used by Tier A, Tier B, paper trading and live. When the two engines are configured identically they agree to 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁶ relative — machine epsilon.

The Strategy interface

A strategy is deliberately tiny:

def target_weights(self, view: MarketView) -> dict[str, float]:
    ...

It receives what it may see and returns what it wants to hold — target weights, not orders. It does not fetch data (so it cannot reach past as_of), does not place orders (so the same object works in backtest and live), and does not compute costs (so there is exactly one cost path in the system).

4.4 The engine self-tests — the most important gate

Before trusting any strategy result, you must trust the engine. make test-engine runs ten checks on synthetic worlds where the right answer is known in advance:

  • Accounting invariants hold after every single event.
  • Zero alpha = exact cost drag. A strategy with no edge must lose exactly

the modelled costs. It loses ₹78,113.65 over 365 fills, matching to the paisa.

  • Known alpha is recovered. A planted 67.78% edge is recovered as 67.41%.
  • Absent alpha is not invented. A no-edge strategy earns nothing.
  • Determinism. Repeat runs are byte-identical.
  • Golden file. Output matches a committed reference exactly.

There is also a mutation suite: deliberate bugs are injected to confirm the tests actually catch them. This found a real hole — the zero-alpha identity does not catch double-charged costs, because both sides of the identity double. An independent recomputation was added to catch it.

Part III — Validation: catching self-deception

5.1 Cross-validation that does not leak

Standard k-fold cross-validation is invalid for time series. Two reasons:

  1. Lookahead. Training on 2020 to predict 2018 is meaningless.
  2. Leakage through overlapping labels. If your label is a 21-day forward

return, then a training sample from day 100 and a test sample from day 110 share 11 days of the same future. They are not independent.

The fix is Combinatorial Purged Cross-Validation (CPCV):

  • Split time into n contiguous blocks (default 6).
  • Choose every combination of k blocks as the test set (default 2), giving

C(6,2) = 15 distinct paths.

  • Purge: remove training samples whose label window overlaps the test

period — that is, drop the horizon days immediately adjacent.

  • Embargo: additionally drop a further embargo days (default 5) after

each test block, because serial correlation leaks slightly past the label window.

Multiple paths matter: a single train/test split gives one number, and one number cannot tell you whether your selection process is picking noise.

A real mistake this caught. The first leakage check condemned 14 of 15 correct paths — it was measuring gaps against the real calendar rather than against the filtered timeline of days actually present.

5.2 Sharpe ratio and its moments

The Sharpe ratio is return per unit of volatility, annualised:

              mean(r)
    SR  =  ──────────── × √252
              std(r)

with r the daily returns and 252 trading days per year.

The system also computes skewness and excess kurtosis:

    skew  =  (1/n) Σ (r_i − r̄)³ / σ³

    kurt  =  (1/n) Σ (r_i − r̄)⁴ / σ⁴  −  3

These are not decoration. Most trading strategies have negative skew (many small gains, occasional large losses) and fat tails, and the correction in §5.3 is larger for such streams. Ignoring them understates the correction in the flattering direction.

One configuration in this project still shows excess kurtosis ≈ 155 (T-1310). Fat tails there are not fully explained.

5.3 The Deflated Sharpe Ratio — the heart of the matter

Return to §1. If you try N strategies, what Sharpe should the best worthless one show? Bailey & López de Prado's approximation to the expected maximum of N independent standard normals:

    SR₀  =  σ_SR · [ (1 − γ)·Φ⁻¹(1 − 1/N)  +  γ·Φ⁻¹(1 − 1/(N·e)) ]

where γ ≈ 0.5772 (the Euler–Mascheroni constant), Φ⁻¹ is the inverse normal CDF, and σ_SR is the standard deviation of Sharpes across trials.

This is the bar to beat. It rises with every strategy you try.

The Deflated Sharpe Ratio then gives the probability that the true Sharpe is above zero, given that N attempts were made:

                   ( SR − SR₀ ) · √(n − 1)
    DSR  =  Φ  ────────────────────────────────────
                √( 1 − skew·SR + (kurt/4)·SR² )

where n is the number of return observations and SR, skew, kurt are in per-period units.

Read it as a probability: DSR = 0.95 means a 95% chance this is not noise. The mandate requires ≥ 0.95.

Note the denominator. Negative skew makes −skew·SR positive, enlarging the denominator and shrinking DSR. Fat tails do the same. A strategy with ugly moments is penalised, correctly.

This project's live numbers. 19 trials are logged. The noise bar is currently Sharpe 1.88. The best result this project has ever produced is 1.27. Read that again: the best strategy found so far is below what pure noise would be expected to produce given how much has been searched.

5.4 Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO)

DSR judges one strategy. PBO judges the act of choosing.

For each CPCV split:

  1. Find the configuration that scored best in sample.
  2. Look up where that same configuration ranks out of sample.
  3. Convert its relative rank ω ∈ (0,1) to a logit:
    λ  =  log( ω / (1 − ω) )

Then:

    PBO  =  fraction of splits where λ ≤ 0

i.e. how often the in-sample winner lands below the out-of-sample median.

  • PBO > 0.5 — worse than a coin flip; your selection is picking noise.
  • PBO > 0.25 — substantial overfitting risk.
  • Mandate allows ≤ 0.25.

This indicts the procedure, not any single configuration. That distinction is the whole point.

5.5 Minimum Backtest Length

The blunt version of the same idea. How many years of history do you need before a Sharpe of SR_target is distinguishable from luck, given N trials?

    MinBTL  =  ( SR₀ / SR_target )²      years

If the answer exceeds the history you actually have, no amount of analysis on that history can support the claim. The mandate requires at least 1.5× the needed length.

5.6 Stress tests: when does it break?

The rest of the battery asks "could this be luck?". Stress asks "what has to stay true?".

testquestionmandate
2× costsDoes the edge survive doubling every charge?≥ 60% of Sharpe retained
Drop best 5 namesIs it a handful of lucky positions?≥ 60% retained
Worst regimeDoes it work in more than one market?≥ 50% retained
Signal stalenessDoes a delay destroy it?must stay positive

A real mistake this caught. The cost-stress test originally scaled only brokerage — which is zero on delivery trades. So "2× costs" quietly meant "introduce a 0.06% fee that did not exist", while STT and stamp duty (the charges that actually dominate) were untouched. It reported 95% of Sharpe surviving 5× costs, which was not a fact about the strategy. The multiplier now applies to the total of every charge.

5.6a Monte Carlo resampling: order versus luck

Two questions the rest of the battery does not ask, and they need different operations (ADR-034):

Shuffle — reorder the same returns. Mean and standard deviation are unchanged, so the Sharpe is identical in every path; reporting a distribution of it would be reporting nothing. What changes is the equity path, because a drawdown depends entirely on the order losses arrive in:

    max_drawdown  =  min over t of  ( E_t / max(E_1..E_t) − 1 )

If the realised drawdown is shallower than the median reshuffled path, the backtest simply has not met its bad ordering yet.

Bootstrap — resample with replacement. This does move the mean and the standard deviation, giving a genuine confidence interval on the edge.

Measured on momentum_12_1 over 700 days: observed Sharpe 0.11, bootstrap 5th percentile −0.96. The data cannot rule out that there is no edge at all.

Both assume returns are exchangeable. Real returns cluster — a crash is a run of bad days, not bad days sprinkled evenly — and shuffling breaks up exactly the clustering that produces the worst drawdowns. So this is a lower bound on risk, never the whole of it. Neither test spends a trial, because resampling an existing result evaluates nothing new.

5.7 The trial ledger: a counter that cannot be quietly edited

Every strategy configuration evaluated appends one entry to data/derived/trial_ledger.jsonl. Each entry contains the hash of the entry before it — a hash chain:

    hash_i  =  SHA256( payload_i  ‖  hash_{i−1} )

Altering or removing any row breaks every link after it. This does not make tampering impossible — anyone can rewrite the whole chain — but it makes it detectable and deliberate rather than quiet.

Why this file specifically? Because every statistic in §5.3–5.5 gets more favourable as the trial count falls. DSR rises. MinBTL shrinks. The multiple-testing correction weakens. The one number the entire battery rests on is also the one with the strongest incentive to shrink, and "we promised not to touch it" is not a control.

The validation battery refuses to report anything if the chain is broken.

A real incident, recorded. On 2026-08-20, make gate H=H-0001 was run merely to check the command still worked. It logged trial #19 — a second evaluation of an already-killed hypothesis. The bar moved from Sharpe 1.83 to 1.88, permanently, for every future candidate. There is no un-spend function and there should not be (ticket T-2609).

5.8 The promotion gate

gates.py and mandate.yaml are operator-owned. The AI that built this system may propose changes in a review; it may not edit them.

The separation is structural:

  • mandate.yaml holds every threshold and no logic.
  • gates.py holds logic and no thresholds. A test enforces that no numeric

literal appears in it.

So a threshold cannot be relaxed as a side effect of a refactor. Changing what the gate demands means editing a YAML file the operator owns, visibly, on its own.

Three more properties:

  1. The default answer is no. A check that cannot be evaluated fails. A

missing field fails. Wrongly killing a good strategy costs an opportunity; wrongly promoting a bad one costs money.

  1. The verdict is binary. PROMOTE or KILL. There is no "pass with

concerns", because a gate that can express reservations will be overruled by whoever reads them.

  1. A strategy that fails is dead. It is not tuned and resubmitted.

Resubmitting under a new ID is visible in the ledger, which is what the ledger is for.

The holdout is a slice of data read exactly once, by the operator's action, only after a PROMOTE. There is no un-spend function. A KILL verdict refuses the read entirely.

Acceptance: three deliberately overfit strategies are fed to the gate; all three must die. They do — killed on deflated Sharpe, PBO and worst-regime.

Part IV — Execution: from decision to order

6.1 The order state machine

The core insight: a send has three outcomes, not two. It landed, it did not land, or we do not know.

      INTENT ──send ok──► SENT ──ack──► ACKNOWLEDGED
        │                  │                 │
        │                  ├──fill──────────►├──fill──► FILLED ●
        │                  ├──reject────────►├─reject─► REJECTED ●
        │  refused         │                 └─cancel─► CANCELLED ●
        ├──locally──► UNSENT ●               │
        │                  │                 │
        └──ambiguous──► UNKNOWN ◄────restart─┘
                           │
                           │  ONLY a broker query leaves here
                           ├── query found resting  ──► ACKNOWLEDGED
                           ├── query found filled   ──► FILLED ●
                           ├── query absent         ──► UNSENT ●
                           └── query FAILED         ──► UNKNOWN  (stays)

                                              ● = terminal

UNKNOWN is a state, not an error. A network timeout, a connection reset, a process killed mid-send — all leave an order that may or may not exist at the exchange. Systems that model two outcomes handle the third by guessing, and guessing wrong costs money in both directions:

  • Assume it failed → retry → you are double-sent.
  • Assume it worked → you are short a position you think you hold.

So QUERY_FAILED maps UNKNOWN back to UNKNOWN. There is deliberately no timeout after which the system gives up. A test asserts this over the entire transition table, not over chosen examples.

UNSENT ≠ REJECTED. Both terminal, opposite meanings: UNSENT means the exchange has no record; REJECTED means it saw the order and refused.

6.2 The write-ahead journal

**The intent is written to disk and fsynced before the network call.**

Reverse those two lines and a crash in between leaves an order at the exchange that no local record mentions — invisible to reconciliation forever, because reconciliation compares against a list that does not contain it. That is the one failure mode with no recovery path at all.

The journal is append-only JSONL with strictly increasing sequence numbers (gaps reveal deletion). A torn final line — a crash mid-write — is dropped, because that event never completed. A malformed line anywhere else raises, because it cannot have come from a crash. A damaged journal must never read as an empty one.

6.3 The rate governor

max_orders_per_second (default 5) is read from config, never hardcoded.

It is implemented as a sliding one-second window, not a token bucket. The distinction is not academic: a bucket refilling 5 tokens per second permits 5 sends at 0.99s and 5 more at 1.01s — ten orders inside 200 milliseconds, which is precisely the burst the rule exists to prevent.

6.4 Reconciliation: the broker is right

Local position state is a cache. The broker is the source of truth.

Taking that literally has one consequence people flinch at: the fix is always to the local record, never to the market. The reconciler will rewrite what we think we hold. It will never place an order to make the broker match our belief, and there is no flag to enable it.

Why? A break means we do not know what happened, and the commonest cause is a fill we failed to record. Trading the difference doubles the position exactly when we are least able to tell.

Four break types, worst first:

kindmeaning
broker_onlya position we have no record of — unmanaged risk
quantityboth know the symbol, sizes disagree
local_onlywe think we hold it, the broker does not
unknown_orderan order working at the broker we never sent

6.5 Kill switches

Anything may trip it. Almost nothing may clear it. Tripping is cheap and reversible — the cost of a false trip is a day of not trading. Clearing re-arms a system that something just decided was misbehaving.

  • trip() is importable and needs no credential.
  • There is no reset function anywhere in the importable surface. Clearing

happens behind a CLI flag and requires KILLSWITCH_TOKEN, a variable the trading process never loads. A test asserts no other module in src/ even names it.

  • The first trip reason survives later trips — later ones are consequences

of having already stopped.

  • An unreadable trip file still reads as tripped. Failing to parse must never

mean "clear".

Automatic limits: daily loss, orders per day, consecutive rejections, position notional, and unresolved (UNKNOWN) orders.

Honest limitation (S-019). On one machine with one user account, a process can delete the trip file directly. The separation is by code structure and credential, not by the operating system. Real separation means a different user on a different host, and a second broker API key whose only power is to square off and disable — so the switch works when this machine is the broken thing.

6.6 Chaos testing

make chaos runs eleven scenarios. Two of them genuinely SIGKILL a real subprocess mid-order and then ask a fresh process to work out what happened from the files on disk:

scenariothe question
killed before the senddo we invent a position never placed?
killed after the senddo we lose a position that was placed?
ambiguous senddo we guess, or go and ask?
lost sendsame event, opposite truth — do we tell them apart?
retrydoes sending twice send twice?
broker downdoes an unanswerable question get answered anyway?
rate limitis the governor a real bound or an average?
kill switchdoes tripped mean tripped?
out-of-band positiondo we notice risk we did not create?
corrupt journaldoes a damaged record read as an empty one?

SIGKILL rather than an exception, because exceptions unwind: finally blocks run, buffers flush. A power cut does none of that.

6.7 Paper trading and the reality gap

There is no paper-trading loop. There is the Tier B engine with a different Executor plugged in — OMSExecutor instead of SimulatedExecutor. That is the entire difference.

The consequence is strong: a frictionless paper run must reproduce the backtest exactly. Measured on the real panel over 546 days and 829 trades, the gap is 0.00. If it were ever non-zero, the two paths have drifted, and that is a defect to fix rather than a market fact to explain.

The reality gap decomposes into five terms:

    gap  =  slippage
         +  cost charged on a different price
         +  trades the backtest made and paper did not
         +  trades paper made and the backtest did not
         +  position divergence, marked to market

What the residual does and does not prove (S-018). Both books are ours, so this decomposition is an identity and closes to zero by arithmetic. It proves the arithmetic, not that live execution is understood. The build plan's "residual under 10%" target concerns a live shortfall and cannot exist until there is a real counterparty.

Part V — The workbench

7.1 Three ways to author a strategy

The web interface (make workbench) offers three routes, all producing the same Strategy object:

  1. Describe — plain English → a language model drafts a spec.
  2. Compose — pick a feature, a holding count and a rebalance period.
  3. Code — write weights(view) directly.

7.2 The sandbox: how "paste code" stays safe

Executing arbitrary Python would discard the lookahead firewall, because arbitrary code can open the panel file directly instead of going through a MarketView.

The insight is that the danger was never executing code — it was executing code with reach. Three layers remove it:

  1. Allowlisted AST. import, while, class, try, with, global,

del, decorators, generators and async are rejected before compilation. Anything not on the list is rejected too; the default is no.

  1. Allowlisted attribute names. Not merely "no dunders" — a name must be on

the list to be readable at all. This closes ().__class__.__bases__[0].__subclasses__() and every variant.

  1. Replaced builtins. open, getattr, eval, exec, type,

__import__ and globals do not exist. No name in scope returns a module, a class, or a file.

Names are resolved at submit time, so open(...) fails immediately with "open is not available" rather than as a NameError hundreds of rebalances into a run.

Not solved (S-021): this stops code reaching data it should not see. It is not a defence against a hostile adversary — a determined person can still write an expression that runs for a very long time. while is banned and range is capped, but the real protection against a runaway strategy is the wall-clock timeout imposed from outside.

7.3 The language model is the least trusted component

  • It drafts; it does not decide. Output is validated by code that can

reject it. A hallucinated feature name produces an error listing the ten that actually exist.

  • The operator sees it before it runs. Drafting starts no backtest.
  • Provider-agnostic. Any OpenAI-compatible endpoint; the default is local

Ollama, which is free, has no quota and sends nothing anywhere.

Neither mode can produce lookahead — not because the prompt says so, but because the MarketView contains no future rows to reach.

7.4 What the interface refuses to do

  • It never spends a trial. A web UI makes running things cheap, which is

exactly what would let someone burn fifty trials in an afternoon by clicking.

  • It never promotes anything. There is no route to the gate.
  • It never runs unvalidated code.

Every result carries its caveats in the payload, so the warning travels with the number rather than being a paragraph someone can style away.

Part VI — Verifying it yourself

Two commands, and they answer different questions.

make verify        # is the repository healthy?  7 checks, ~922 tests
make acceptance    # does each sprint still work on real data?  11 checks

verify covers lint, types, tests, docs, the engine gate and the chaos gate. acceptance runs each sprint's own acceptance command against the real panel — a distinction that matters, because every unit test can pass while a data artefact is stale.

Useful individually:

make chaos                                # kill it mid-order, 11 scenarios
make test-engine                          # the ten engine self-tests
make check-adjust                         # corporate-action mathematics
make gate-acceptance                      # 3 overfit strategies must all die
make attribution FRICTIONLESS=1 DAYS=800  # gap must be exactly 0.00
make reconcile-book                       # our positions vs the broker's

Two commands cost something. make gate and make validate each write a permanent trial to the ledger and raise the bar for every future strategy. Neither is in verify or acceptance, deliberately.

Glossary

Adjustment factor — the divisor that makes prices before a corporate action comparable with prices after it.

Backtest — simulating a strategy on historical data.

Bitemporal — storing both when something happened and when it became knowable.

Breakdown point — the fraction of data that can be arbitrarily wrong before a statistic becomes meaningless. MAD: 0.5. Standard deviation: 0.

CPCV — Combinatorial Purged Cross-Validation. Time-series-safe validation using multiple train/test paths with purging and embargo.

DSR — Deflated Sharpe Ratio. The probability a Sharpe is real, adjusted for how many things were tried.

Embargo — extra days dropped after a test block to stop serial correlation leaking.

Holdout — data reserved and read exactly once, at the end.

IC — Information Coefficient. Rank correlation between a feature and the return that followed.

Idempotent — doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once.

Lookahead bias — using information that was not available at the time.

MAD — Median Absolute Deviation, a robust measure of spread.

MinBTL — Minimum Backtest Length. Years of history needed before a Sharpe is distinguishable from luck.

OMS / EMS — Order Management System (tracks orders) / Execution Management System (sends them, enforces rate limits).

PBO — Probability of Backtest Overfitting. How often the in-sample winner underperforms out of sample.

Purging — removing training samples whose label window overlaps the test period.

Sharpe ratio — return per unit of volatility, annualised.

Slippage — the difference between the price you expected and the price you got.

STT — Securities Transaction Tax, an Indian statutory charge on trades.

Survivorship bias — testing on today's list of companies, which excludes everything that failed.

TERP — Theoretical Ex-Rights Price, the correct divisor for a rights issue.

Trial — one strategy configuration evaluated. Counted forever.
